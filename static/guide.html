What CountGPT is

A practice tool, not a stamp of approval

CountGPT is a study partner for people learning government-style cybersecurity paperwork. You ask a question. It looks up the real wording of a security rule, then writes an answer you can read and edit.

It is not an official decision-maker. It cannot give a system permission to go live. It cannot replace a human reviewer who signs papers for your organization.

What you can practice here

Use Chat to look up a rule or ask what a term means. Use the Workbench when you want a structured draft of a fix-list item or a “how we protect this system” paragraph.

Treat every answer as homework, not as a finished package. If the retrieved rule on the right is the wrong one, the draft will be wrong too.

NIST SP 800-53 in plain English

A catalog of security rules

is a U.S. government lab that publishes how-to documents for technology. Special Publication 800-53 is one of those documents: a catalog of security rules.

Each rule is called a . A control is a specific thing you must do to protect a system — for example, manage user accounts, or keep logs of important events.

IDs, families, and extra steps

You will see IDs like AC-2. The letters are a (AC means Access Control). The number is the specific rule.

Some rules have add-ons called , written like AC-2(1). Those are extra steps on top of the main rule. Whether you must do them depends on how sensitive the system is.

RMF & ATO

Permission to operate

Before many government systems are allowed to run, someone with authority has to say “yes, the leftover risk is acceptable.” That written permission is an — Authority to Operate.

The process used to get there is the — Risk Management Framework. CountGPT can help you practice the writing and lookup parts. It does not grant an ATO.

The high-level steps

  1. Describe what you are protecting and how sensitive it is.
  2. Pick the security rules that apply.
  3. Write down how you will follow those rules.
  4. Test whether you actually follow them.
  5. A person in charge accepts leftover risk and grants or denies the ATO.
  6. Keep watching the system after it is live.

SSP vs POA&M vs SAR

Three documents that travel together

These three papers usually sit in the same authorization package — the folder of evidence used to ask for permission to operate.

The (System Security Plan) is the “how we protect this system” story. For each rule, it says what is in place today.

The (Plan of Action and Milestones) is the “what is still broken, and when we will fix it” list. Each item has an owner, a due date, and a status.

The (Security Assessment Report) is the “what testers found” report. Independent reviewers write what passed, what failed, and how serious each miss is.

How they connect

Testers write findings in the SAR. Open problems become POA&M items. The SSP should stay honest about what is implemented versus what is still being fixed. If those three stories disagree, a reviewer will notice.

Roles: ISSO, ISSM, AO

Who does the daily work

The (Information System Security Officer) is the day-to-day security person for a system. They track findings, keep documents current, and help engineers fix problems.

The (Information System Security Manager) is a step above. They oversee security across one or more systems and support the ISSO.

Who accepts the leftover risk

The (Authorizing Official) is the person who accepts leftover risk and signs the ATO. They are the decision-maker, not the person writing the drafts.

In many shops the ISSO drafts, the ISSM reviews, and the AO decides. Titles vary. The idea does not: writing help is not the same as authorization.

Severity & fix timelines

How serious is the problem?

When a problem is found, it usually gets a label: High, Moderate, or Low. That label is a judgment about how much harm the problem could cause.

Common practice — not universal law

A common and Department of Defense-style practice is to aim for these fix windows from the day the problem was found:

  • High: 30 days
  • Moderate: 90 days
  • Low: 180 days

Your organization may use different clocks. and accepted risk can change the plan. Always check your own policy. The Workbench uses these windows as a starting template only.

ACAS vs STIG

Two different kinds of findings

is a scanning program used in the Department of Defense. In practice people often mean a Nessus-style vulnerability scan: the tool checks computers for known holes and reports plugin IDs.

A (Security Technical Implementation Guide) is a configuration checklist. It says how a product should be set up — which settings should be on or off.

Do not mix them up in a write-up

A scan finding (“this software version has a known hole”) is different from a STIG finding (“this setting is not hardened”). Both can become POA&M items. When you describe the weakness, say which kind of check produced it, and do not invent a plugin number you do not have.

How CountGPT answers

Look up real rule text first

When you ask a question, CountGPT first searches a local copy of the real NIST SP 800-53 control text. That search is called retrieval. Matching rule IDs and short excerpts show up in the Sources panel.

Then it drafts an answer using those retrieved rules. If you asked for a POA&M or SSP paragraph, it writes a draft you can edit. Learning questions (what is an SSP, who is the ISSO) are answered as teaching, not as official policy.

Always review

Read the sources. If the retrieved control is wrong, the draft will be wrong. Never paste a draft into an official package without a human review. The yellow banner is there on purpose.
